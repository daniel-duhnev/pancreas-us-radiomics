\chapter{Results}

\section{Feature Extraction Summary}
\label{sec:feature_extraction_results}

Radiomics feature extraction was applied to all 137 studies in the preprocessed dataset. The extraction completed without errors for every study, yielding a total of 93 features per study across the six feature classes described in Section~\ref{sec:feature_extraction}: first-order statistics (18 features), GLCM (24), GLRLM (16), GLSZM (16), GLDM (14), and NGTDM (5).

An analysis of inter-feature correlations revealed substantial multicollinearity among the extracted features. A total of 316 feature pairs exhibited absolute Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.9 (Figure~\ref{fig:correlation_heatmap}). The highest correlations occurred within feature classes---particularly among the GLRLM and GLSZM families, where many features capture related aspects of texture run-length and zone-size distributions. To reduce redundancy prior to machine learning classification, a correlation-based filter was applied (described in Section~\ref{sec:ml_classification}), reducing the feature set from 93 to 27 features.

\section{Radiomics Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:radiomics_results}

The univariate statistical tests were performed primarily on the independent dataset of 55 studies (one study per patient, 34 no rejection and 21 rejection), which satisfies the independence assumption underlying the tests (Section~\ref{sec:statistical_analysis}). Based on Shapiro-Wilk tests, 45 features were analysed with Welch's $t$-test and 48 with the Mann-Whitney $U$ test.

Twenty-four features reached nominal significance at $p < 0.05$, predominantly from the first-order statistics class (7 of 24). The top-ranked features were \texttt{firstorder\_90Percentile} ($p = 0.004$, rank-biserial $r = -0.46$), \texttt{firstorder\_RootMeanSquared} ($p = 0.005$, $r = -0.46$), and \texttt{firstorder\_Mean} ($p = 0.005$, $r = -0.45$). In 19 of the 24 significant features, the rejection group showed lower values, suggesting a weak trend toward lower image intensities in rejection cases. However, none survived FDR correction (all adjusted $p \geq 0.16$), and the number of nominally significant features (24 of 93, 26\%) only modestly exceeds the 5\% expected under the null hypothesis when considering the correlation structure among features. The independent dataset thus rules out within-patient correlation as the explanation for the negative result: even when each patient contributes exactly one observation, no radiomics feature achieves statistically significant discrimination after correction for multiple testing. The complete 93-feature table for the independent dataset is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:full_features}. An exploratory analysis on the full dataset of 137 studies, which does not satisfy the independence assumption, is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:full_dataset} and yields the same negative conclusion.

\subsection{Time-Stratified Analysis}

To test whether discrimination emerges within a specific post-transplant period, the independent dataset was split by days post-transplant into an early group ($\leq 90$ days, $n = 42$: 28 no rejection, 14 rejection) and a late group ($> 90$ days, $n = 13$: 6 no rejection, 7 rejection), using the same 90-day cutoff as the clinical analysis (Section~\ref{sec:clinical_results}). In the early group, a single feature reached uncorrected significance (\texttt{firstorder\_RootMeanSquared}, $p = 0.044$). In the late group, a single feature reached uncorrected significance (\texttt{firstorder\_Maximum}, $p = 0.038$). Neither survived FDR correction (minimum adjusted $p = 0.61$ in the early group and $0.34$ in the late group), and the two nominally significant features differ between subgroups, indicating chance findings rather than a consistent time-dependent signal. The late subgroup contains only 13 studies and is therefore underpowered, so this comparison is best regarded as a robustness check. Its result is consistent with the primary independent-dataset analysis. The corresponding motivo-based stratification of the full dataset is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:full_dataset}.

\subsection{Late Post-Transplant Period}

The clinical feature analysis was repeated on the late post-transplant subset using the days-based stratification ($> 90$ days post-transplant, $n = 58$: 36 no rejection, 22 rejection), which matches the cutoff used by Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025}. In contrast to the full dataset, eight features reached significance at $p < 0.05$ (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_late}). All four ARFI features were highly significant, with ARFI media and ARFI mediana reaching $p < 0.001$. The rejection group showed substantially higher shear wave velocities (median 1.44--1.46~m/s vs 0.97~m/s for no rejection), indicating increased tissue stiffness consistent with chronic rejection-related fibrosis. Four DCE-US perfusion parameters also reached significance (WiAUC, WoAUC, WiWoAUC, WiPi), with the rejection group showing lower perfusion values.

The early post-transplant period ($\leq 90$ days, $n = 80$) showed no significant features, consistent with the clinical observation that early post-operative variability obscures rejection-related differences.

\subsection{Replication of Bassaganyas et al.}

Table~\ref{tab:replication} compares our late-period $p$-values with those reported by Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025} for the 12 DCE-US and ARFI features reported in their publication. Using the same $> 90$-day cutoff and the same dataset, our results match the published values to three decimal places for 10 of the 12 features. The ARFI mediana $p$-value is reported as ``$< 0.001$'' in both analyses. The only notable discrepancy is RT (rise time), where our $p$-value (0.142) is lower than the published value (0.276). However, both are non-significant and this difference does not affect the conclusions. The most likely explanation is that the rise time values in the clinical database were updated between the original analysis and our data export---RT is computed from the initial slope of the time-intensity curve and is the DCE-US parameter most sensitive to curve-fitting parameters, whereas integral and peak measures (which match exactly) are more robust to fitting choices.

This near-perfect replication serves two purposes. First, it validates our analysis pipeline: the data loading, preprocessing, group assignment, and statistical testing produce results consistent with an independent analysis of the same dataset. Second, it confirms that the negative radiomics result (Section~\ref{sec:radiomics_results}) is not an artifact of a flawed pipeline---the same pipeline detects the clinical biomarker signal when it exists.

\subsection{Independent Patient Analysis}

To assess whether the clinical biomarkers discriminate rejection under the same independence constraint applied to the radiomics analysis, the 17 clinical features were tested on the independent dataset of 55 studies (one study per patient, 34 no rejection and 21 rejection). ARFI measurements were available for 47 of these studies and DCE-US measurements for 54. In contrast to the full-dataset and late-period analyses, no clinical feature reached significance, even before correction (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_independent}). The ARFI features, which were highly significant in the late post-transplant period, were far from significant here (ARFI mediana $p = 0.69$, ARFI media $p = 0.77$). The smallest $p$-value across all 17 features was 0.060 (RT, rise time).

This null result does not contradict the positive late-period finding. Rather, it reflects the time dependence of the ARFI signal. The independent dataset pools studies across all post-transplant periods and, for patients with both outcomes, preferentially selects the first rejection study, which frequently occurs early. Of the 21 rejection studies in the independent dataset, only 7 fall in the late ($> 90$ day) period where ARFI discrimination is concentrated. Pooling early and late studies therefore dilutes the ARFI effect, consistent with the paired analysis (Section~\ref{sec:paired_results}), which likewise found the within-patient ARFI difference to be non-significant.

\section{Machine Learning Classification}
\label{sec:ml_results}

Four classifiers were trained and evaluated on the independent dataset of 55 studies (one study per patient, 34 no rejection and 21 rejection) using the joint optimisation pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:ml_classification}. The correlation-based filter reduced the feature set from 93 to 27 features on this dataset. Evaluation used 10-fold stratified cross-validation as the primary metric, with LOOCV as a secondary comparison. Table~\ref{tab:ml_independent} presents the results.

Performance remained near chance. Logistic Regression achieved the highest 10-fold CV AUC of 0.636, with a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval of [0.48, 0.78] that includes 0.5. SVM collapsed entirely (AUC $= 0.408$, sensitivity $= 1.0$, specificity $= 0.029$), predicting nearly all studies as rejection---a sign of model instability with small samples. Naive Bayes achieved AUC $= 0.618$ [0.45, 0.78], also spanning chance.

\section{Paired Analysis}
\label{sec:paired_results}

The preceding analyses treat each study as an independent observation, which is appropriate for replicating the methodology of Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025} but does not account for within-patient correlation. To address this, a paired analysis was conducted on the 14 patients who contributed both rejection and no-rejection studies to the dataset.

\subsection{Pair Selection}

For each of the 14 patients, one rejection study and one no-rejection study were selected to form a matched pair. The selection criterion maximised the study-number gap between the two studies, ensuring that the paired observations represent distinct clinical episodes rather than closely spaced follow-ups. The resulting pairs had a median gap of 2 study visits (range 1--5). Table~\ref{tab:paired_summary} summarises the available sample sizes.

Notably, the features reaching nominal significance in the paired analysis differ from those in the unpaired analyses (Section~\ref{sec:radiomics_results}). In the full-dataset analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:full_dataset}), \texttt{firstorder\_Minimum} ranked highest ($p = 0.022$). By contrast, the paired analysis is dominated by gray-level-related features rather than first-order intensity features, and \texttt{ngtdm\_Coarseness} appears as the top hit. This instability across analysis approaches further confirms the absence of a reproducible signal rather than a consistent but weak effect.

\subsection{Clinical Features}

The same paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to the 17 clinical imaging features. No feature reached significance at $p < 0.05$. The ARFI features---which showed strong significance in the unpaired late-period analysis (Section~\ref{sec:clinical_results}, $p < 0.001$)---were markedly non-significant in the paired comparison: ARFI mediana ($p = 0.86$), ARFI media ($p = 0.91$), ARFI DE ($p = 0.73$), ARFI RIQ ($p = 0.85$). The best-performing clinical feature was WoR (wash-out rate, $p = 0.16$), which was non-significant in both unpaired analyses.

This result has an important interpretation. The ARFI signal detected in the unpaired analysis likely reflects stable between-patient differences in tissue stiffness---patients who eventually experience rejection may have inherently stiffer grafts---rather than within-patient changes caused by acute rejection episodes. When each patient serves as their own control, the ARFI difference disappears. This does not invalidate ARFI as a clinical biomarker (it still discriminates patients at population level), but it suggests that the mechanism is not a transient rejection-induced stiffness increase detectable by comparing the same patient's studies over time.

\section{Alternative Texture Features}
\label{sec:alternative_results}

To assess whether the negative radiomics finding was specific to the PyRadiomics feature set, 153 alternative texture features were extracted from the same preprocessed images: 54 Local Binary Pattern (LBP) histogram features, 54 Gabor filter response statistics, and 45 Laws' texture energy measures (see Section~\ref{sec:alternative_features} for details).

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

On the full dataset (137 studies), 5 of 153 features reached nominal significance at $p < 0.05$ (uncorrected): three LBP features (\texttt{lbp\_r3\_p24\_bin6} at $p = 0.014$, \texttt{lbp\_r3\_p24\_bin18} at $p = 0.040$, and \texttt{lbp\_r3\_p24\_bin25} at $p = 0.047$) and two Gabor features (\texttt{gabor\_w10\_o150\_std} at $p = 0.036$ and \texttt{gabor\_w5\_o90\_mean} at $p = 0.049$). None survived FDR correction (minimum $q = 0.60$). Under the null hypothesis of no association, 7.7 features ($153 \times 0.05$) would be expected to reach $p < 0.05$ by chance. The observed count of 5 is below this expectation.

On the independent dataset (55 studies, one per patient), 7 features reached uncorrected $p < 0.05$, predominantly from the Laws' family (5 of 7). The strongest was \texttt{laws\_L5W5\_mean} ($p = 0.021$). Again, none survived FDR correction (minimum $q = 0.54$).

\subsection{Machine Learning}

When all 153 alternative features were combined into the joint optimisation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:ml_classification}), the best-performing model was Random Forest (AUC $= 0.55$, 95\% CI [0.40, 0.71]), with $k = 5$ selected features. All four classifiers produced confidence intervals spanning 0.5, confirming the absence of a reliable multivariate signal. The per-family analysis showed that Laws' features achieved the highest individual AUC of 0.651, though its confidence interval [0.50, 0.80] still includes chance performance.

These results are consistent with the PyRadiomics analysis: no robust texture signal discriminates rejection from non-rejection in this cohort. The fact that three methodologically distinct feature families---each capturing different aspects of local texture---all fail to achieve significant discrimination rules out ``wrong features'' as an explanation for the negative finding.


\section{Surrounding Tissue Analysis}
\label{sec:surrounding_results}

To test whether inter-patient variability in acquisition settings confounds the texture analysis, the surrounding-tissue normalisation approach described in Section~\ref{sec:surrounding_tissue} was applied. Two feature sets were evaluated: 93 PyRadiomics features re-extracted from the normalised images, and 11 contrast features quantifying the intensity relationship between the pancreas and surrounding tissue.

\subsection{Normalised Radiomics}

After $z$-score normalisation using the surrounding tissue ring, none of the 93 re-extracted PyRadiomics features reached statistical significance at $p < 0.05$ on the full dataset. The closest was \texttt{gldm\_SmallDependenceLowGrayLevelEmphasis} ($p = 0.057$). All FDR-corrected $q$-values exceeded 0.78. This result is weaker than the standard per-image normalised analysis on the same full dataset (which yielded 4 uncorrected hits; Appendix~\ref{app:full_dataset}), suggesting that the surrounding-tissue normalisation procedure removed rather than revealed discriminative texture information.

Machine learning classification on the surrounding-tissue normalised features confirmed this pattern. The best model (Logistic Regression, $k = 20$) achieved AUC $= 0.525$, marginally above chance and comparable to the standard per-image normalised analysis on the same full dataset, which was likewise near chance (best AUC $0.564$; Appendix~\ref{app:full_dataset}).

\subsection{Contrast Features}

The 11 contrast features---measuring intensity differences and ratios between the pancreas graft and surrounding tissue---showed no significant associations with rejection status. The strongest feature was \texttt{contrast\_p25\_diff} ($p = 0.10$, $q = 0.40$). No feature reached $p < 0.05$.

The best machine learning model using contrast features alone was Naive Bayes (AUC $= 0.549$, $k = 6$), again indistinguishable from chance. Table~\ref{tab:surrounding_ml} summarises the ML results for both feature sets.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The surrounding tissue analysis rules out acquisition variability as the primary confound responsible for the negative radiomics result. If inter-patient differences in gain, depth, or probe pressure were masking a real texture signal, then normalising by the surrounding tissue---which is subject to the same acquisition conditions---should have improved discrimination. Instead, performance remained at chance or slightly decreased. This suggests that the absence of discriminative texture features reflects a genuine lack of detectable tissue microstructure differences between rejection and non-rejection ultrasound images in this cohort.
