\chapter{Results}

\section{Feature Extraction Summary}
\label{sec:feature_extraction_results}

Radiomics feature extraction was applied to all 137 studies in the preprocessed dataset. The extraction completed without errors for every study, yielding a total of 93 features per study across the six feature classes described in Section~\ref{sec:feature_extraction}: first-order statistics (18 features), GLCM (24), GLRLM (16), GLSZM (16), GLDM (14), and NGTDM (5).

An analysis of inter-feature correlations revealed substantial multicollinearity among the extracted features. A total of 300 feature pairs exhibited absolute Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.9 (Figure~\ref{fig:correlation_heatmap}). The highest correlations occurred within feature classes---particularly among the GLRLM and GLSZM families, where many features capture related aspects of texture run-length and zone-size distributions. To reduce redundancy prior to machine learning classification, a correlation-based filter was applied (described in Section~\ref{sec:ml_classification}), reducing the feature set from 93 to 32 features.

\section{Radiomics Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:radiomics_results}

\subsection{Full Dataset}

Univariate statistical tests were performed on all 93 radiomics features using the full dataset of 137 studies (98 no rejection, 39 rejection). Based on the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, 13 features were analysed with Welch's $t$-test and 80 with the Mann-Whitney $U$ test.

No feature reached nominal significance at $p < 0.05$ before multiple testing correction. The closest feature was \texttt{firstorder\_Minimum} (Mann-Whitney $U$, $p = 0.053$, rank-biserial $r = -0.21$), followed by \texttt{firstorder\_10Percentile} ($p = 0.133$) and \texttt{glrlm\_RunEntropy} ($p = 0.159$). After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, all adjusted $p$-values exceeded 0.84, far from the significance threshold. Effect sizes were uniformly small across all features. Table~\ref{tab:radiomics_top10} lists the ten features with the lowest uncorrected $p$-values.

\subsection{Stratified Analysis}

The analysis was repeated on two subsets stratified by the \texttt{motivo} variable (see Section~\ref{sec:statistical_analysis}). In the early period (motivo 1--2, $n = 67$: 57 no rejection, 10 rejection), no feature reached $p < 0.05$; the lowest $p$-value was 0.137 (\texttt{glcm\_ClusterProminence}). In the late period (motivo 3--5, $n = 70$: 41 no rejection, 29 rejection), one feature reached nominal significance---\texttt{firstorder\_Minimum} ($p = 0.037$, rank-biserial $r = -0.30$)---but this did not survive FDR correction across the 93 tests. The late-period result for this feature is consistent with its borderline status in the full dataset analysis.

\subsection{Independent Dataset}

To address the potential confound of repeated measures, the same analysis was performed on the independent dataset of 55 studies (one per patient; 34 no rejection, 21 rejection). Four features reached nominal significance at $p < 0.05$: \texttt{ngtdm\_Busyness} ($p = 0.024$), \texttt{ngtdm\_Strength} ($p = 0.025$), \texttt{glszm\_SmallAreaHighGrayLevelEmphasis} ($p = 0.033$), and \texttt{glrlm\_ShortRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis} ($p = 0.033$). However, none survived FDR correction (all adjusted $p \approx 0.38$). The features ranking highest in the independent dataset differ entirely from those in the full dataset, indicating instability rather than a reproducible signal. The independent dataset thus rules out within-patient correlation as the explanation for the negative result: even when each patient contributes exactly one observation, no radiomics feature discriminates rejection from no rejection.

\section{Clinical Feature Replication}
\label{sec:clinical_results}

\subsection{Full Dataset}

The 17 clinical imaging features (4 ARFI elastography and 13 DCE-US perfusion parameters) were tested on the full clinical dataset of 138 studies (98 no rejection, 40 rejection), which includes study 47\_01 that was excluded from the radiomics analysis due to missing images but had valid clinical measurements. Mann-Whitney $U$ tests were used throughout, consistent with the methodology of Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025}.

Two features reached significance at $p < 0.05$: ARFI mediana ($p = 0.028$, rank-biserial $r = 0.26$) and ARFI media ($p = 0.029$, $r = 0.26$). The rejection group showed higher median shear wave velocity (1.47~m/s vs 1.26~m/s for ARFI mediana), indicating greater tissue stiffness. No DCE-US perfusion parameter reached significance on the full dataset, consistent with the findings of Bassaganyas et al., who reported that perfusion differences emerge only in the late post-transplant period. \subsection{Late Post-Transplant Period}

The clinical feature analysis was repeated on the late post-transplant subset using the days-based stratification ($> 90$ days post-transplant, $n = 58$: 36 no rejection, 22 rejection), which matches the cutoff used by Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025}. In contrast to the full dataset, eight features reached significance at $p < 0.05$ (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_late}). All four ARFI features were highly significant, with ARFI media and ARFI mediana reaching $p < 0.001$. The rejection group showed substantially higher shear wave velocities (median 1.44--1.46~m/s vs 0.97~m/s for no rejection), indicating increased tissue stiffness consistent with chronic rejection-related fibrosis. Four DCE-US perfusion parameters also reached significance (WiAUC, WoAUC, WiWoAUC, WiPi), with the rejection group showing lower perfusion values.

The early post-transplant period ($\leq 90$ days, $n = 80$) showed no significant features, consistent with the clinical observation that early post-operative variability obscures rejection-related differences.

\subsection{Replication of Bassaganyas et al.}

Table~\ref{tab:replication} compares our late-period $p$-values with those reported by Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025} for the 12 DCE-US and ARFI features reported in their publication. Using the same $> 90$-day cutoff and the same dataset, our results match the published values to three decimal places for 10 of the 12 features. The ARFI mediana $p$-value is reported as ``$< 0.001$'' in both analyses. The only notable discrepancy is RT (rise time), where our $p$-value (0.142) is lower than the published value (0.276); however, both are non-significant and this difference does not affect the conclusions.

This near-perfect replication serves two purposes. First, it validates our analysis pipeline: the data loading, preprocessing, group assignment, and statistical testing produce results consistent with an independent analysis of the same dataset. Second, it confirms that the negative radiomics result (Section~\ref{sec:radiomics_results}) is not an artifact of a flawed pipeline---the same pipeline detects the clinical biomarker signal when it exists.

\section{Machine Learning Classification}
\label{sec:ml_results}

\subsection{Full Dataset}

Four classifiers were trained and evaluated on the full dataset of 137 studies using the joint optimisation pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:ml_classification}. The feature set was reduced from 93 to 32 features after correlation-based filtering. Table~\ref{tab:ml_full} summarises the results.

All models achieved AUC values close to 0.5, indicating performance indistinguishable from chance. The best-performing model by LOOCV was Naive Bayes (AUC $= 0.537$), closely followed by SVM (AUC $= 0.531$). The 5-fold cross-validation AUCs were marginally higher (0.51--0.56) due to optimistic bias from the nested hyperparameter tuning, but the LOOCV results provide a more honest estimate of generalisation performance. Figure~\ref{fig:roc_full} shows the LOOCV ROC curves for all four classifiers, which cluster around the diagonal chance line.

The sensitivity--specificity trade-offs further illustrate the lack of signal. Logistic Regression and Random Forest achieved high specificity (0.89--0.92) but very low sensitivity (0.13--0.15), indicating that these models defaulted to predicting the majority class. Naive Bayes showed the opposite pattern (sensitivity $= 0.74$, specificity $= 0.41$), reflecting liberal prediction of rejection. Neither pattern represents clinically useful discrimination.

\subsection{Independent Dataset}

To eliminate the effect of repeated measures, the same pipeline was applied to the independent dataset of 55 studies (one per patient; 34 no rejection, 21 rejection). The correlation-based filter reduced the feature set from 93 to 31 features on this dataset. Evaluation used 10-fold stratified cross-validation as the primary metric, with LOOCV as a secondary comparison. Table~\ref{tab:ml_independent} presents the results.

Performance remained near chance. Logistic Regression achieved the highest 10-fold CV AUC of 0.569, with a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval of [0.40, 0.72] that includes 0.5. SVM collapsed entirely (AUC $= 0.283$, sensitivity $= 1.0$, specificity $= 0.029$), predicting nearly all studies as rejection---a sign of model instability with small samples. Random Forest and Naive Bayes hovered around 0.5.

The consistent near-chance performance across both datasets and multiple classifiers confirms the conclusion from the statistical analysis: radiomics texture features extracted from grayscale ultrasound do not contain discriminative information for pancreas transplant rejection. The ML results rule out the possibility that a multivariate combination of individually non-significant features might achieve useful classification---no such combination was found.

\section{Paired Analysis}
\label{sec:paired_results}

The preceding analyses treat each study as an independent observation, which is appropriate for replicating the methodology of Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025} but does not account for within-patient correlation. To address this, a paired analysis was conducted on the 14 patients who contributed both rejection and no-rejection studies to the dataset.

\subsection{Pair Selection}

For each of the 14 patients, one rejection study and one no-rejection study were selected to form a matched pair. The selection criterion maximised the study-number gap between the two studies, ensuring that the paired observations represent distinct clinical episodes rather than closely spaced follow-ups. The resulting pairs had a median gap of 2 study visits (range 1--5). Table~\ref{tab:paired_summary} summarises the available sample sizes.

\subsection{Radiomics Features}

For each pair, the within-patient difference was computed (rejection value minus no-rejection value) and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to assess whether the median difference differed from zero. Nine of 93 features reached nominal significance at $p < 0.05$, with \texttt{ngtdm\_Coarseness} showing the lowest uncorrected $p$-value ($p = 0.011$, median difference $= -0.001$). However, after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, all adjusted $p$-values equalled 0.28, far from significance. Table~\ref{tab:paired_radiomics} lists the features reaching uncorrected $p < 0.05$.

Notably, the features reaching nominal significance in the paired analysis differ entirely from those in the unpaired analyses (Section~\ref{sec:radiomics_results}). In the full-dataset analysis, \texttt{firstorder\_Minimum} was the closest to significance ($p = 0.053$); in the independent-dataset analysis, \texttt{ngtdm\_Busyness} and \texttt{ngtdm\_Strength} ranked highest. This instability across analysis approaches further confirms the absence of a reproducible signal rather than a consistent but weak effect.

\subsection{Clinical Features}

The same paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to the 17 clinical imaging features. No feature reached significance at $p < 0.05$. The ARFI features---which showed strong significance in the unpaired late-period analysis (Section~\ref{sec:clinical_results}, $p < 0.001$)---were markedly non-significant in the paired comparison: ARFI mediana ($p = 0.86$), ARFI media ($p = 0.91$), ARFI DE ($p = 0.73$), ARFI RIQ ($p = 0.85$). The best-performing clinical feature was WoR (wash-out rate, $p = 0.16$), which was non-significant in both unpaired analyses.

This result has an important interpretation. The ARFI signal detected in the unpaired analysis likely reflects stable between-patient differences in tissue stiffness---patients who eventually experience rejection may have inherently stiffer grafts---rather than within-patient changes caused by acute rejection episodes. When each patient serves as their own control, the ARFI difference disappears. This does not invalidate ARFI as a clinical biomarker (it still discriminates patients at population level), but it suggests that the mechanism is not a transient rejection-induced stiffness increase detectable by comparing the same patient's studies over time.
