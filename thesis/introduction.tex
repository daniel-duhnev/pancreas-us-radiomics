\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Pancreas transplantation is the only established treatment that can restore long-term normoglycaemia in patients with type~1 diabetes mellitus, eliminating the need for exogenous insulin administration and reducing the progression of secondary diabetic complications \cite{gruessner2016, white2009}. Despite advances in surgical technique and immunosuppressive therapy, graft rejection remains a common complication that threatens transplant outcomes \cite{niederhaus2013}. Early detection of rejection is critical, as timely adjustment of immunosuppression can prevent irreversible graft damage and loss \cite{redfield2015}.

The current reference standard for diagnosing pancreas graft rejection is percutaneous biopsy, which provides histological confirmation of immune-mediated tissue injury. However, biopsy is an invasive procedure that carries risks of bleeding, pancreatitis, and fistula formation \cite{klassen2002, singh2024}. It is also subject to sampling variability, as focal rejection may be missed if the biopsy needle does not sample the affected region \cite{drachenberg2008}. For these reasons, biopsy is typically reserved for cases with strong clinical suspicion rather than used as a routine screening tool \cite{singh2024}.

Ultrasound imaging offers a non-invasive alternative for post-transplant surveillance. Conventional B-mode (grayscale) ultrasound is routinely performed to assess graft morphology \cite{monelli2019}, while advanced techniques such as Acoustic Radiation Force Impulse (ARFI) elastography and dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (DCE-US) provide additional information about tissue stiffness and perfusion, respectively. Recent work by Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025} demonstrated that ARFI measurements are significantly associated with rejection status in pancreas transplant recipients, particularly in the late post-transplant period (beyond 90 days). These findings suggest that quantitative imaging biomarkers derived from ultrasound could serve as non-invasive markers of rejection.

However, the clinical imaging biomarkers used in current practice---such as ARFI shear wave velocity and DCE-US perfusion parameters---require specialised acquisition protocols and manual measurement by trained radiologists. This limits their scalability and introduces operator-dependent variability. An automated approach to extracting quantitative information from ultrasound images could potentially overcome these limitations.

Radiomics is a computational approach that extracts large numbers of quantitative features from medical images, capturing patterns of texture, intensity, and spatial heterogeneity that may not be perceptible to the human eye \cite{lambin2012, gillies2016}. Originally developed for oncological applications in computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), radiomics has been increasingly applied to ultrasound imaging in recent years. The key premise is that tissue-level changes associated with disease---such as the inflammation, oedema, and fibrosis characteristic of transplant rejection---may alter the texture patterns visible in grayscale ultrasound images, even when these changes are too subtle for visual assessment.

This thesis investigates whether radiomics texture features extracted from conventional grayscale ultrasound images can discriminate between pancreas transplant rejection and non-rejection. If successful, such an approach could provide an automated, non-invasive complement to existing clinical biomarkers. The study also reproduces the statistical analysis of the clinical imaging biomarkers reported by Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025} on the same patient cohort, allowing a direct comparison between automated radiomics features and manually measured clinical biomarkers.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Pancreas Transplant Rejection Monitoring}

Pancreas transplantation restores endogenous insulin production in patients with type~1 diabetes, but long-term success depends on preventing and detecting graft rejection. Rejection remains the leading cause of graft failure beyond the first 90 days post-transplantation, affecting approximately 20\% of grafts within the first year \cite{niederhaus2013, white2009}. Three main categories are recognised: acute T-cell-mediated rejection, antibody-mediated rejection, and chronic rejection characterised by progressive fibrosis \cite{niederhaus2013, drachenberg2008}. Clinical manifestations may be absent in subclinical rejection, and currently available biomarkers such as serum amylase, lipase, and blood glucose are non-specific, showing irregular correlation with histological rejection \cite{redfield2015}.

The gold standard for diagnosing pancreas graft rejection is percutaneous biopsy with histological grading according to the Banff schema \cite{drachenberg2008}. However, biopsy has significant practical limitations: the graft is not always accessible due to its deep intra-abdominal position, the procedure carries risks of bleeding and pancreatitis, and sampling error means that focal rejection may be missed \cite{klassen2002, singh2024}. A recent multi-centre survey reported that biopsy is performed in only a minority of suspected rejection episodes in US transplant centres, with technical failure rates and complication concerns cited as primary barriers \cite{singh2024}. For these reasons, there is a clear clinical need for reliable non-invasive tools that can identify grafts at risk of rejection and guide the decision to proceed with biopsy.

\subsection{Ultrasound Imaging of Pancreas Grafts}

Ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality for evaluating pancreas graft dysfunction, primarily used to assess vascular patency and rule out surgical complications such as thrombosis, pseudocyst formation, and perigraft collections \cite{monelli2019}. Conventional B-mode (grayscale) ultrasound provides morphological assessment but has limited ability to characterise tissue pathology or distinguish rejection from other causes of graft dysfunction.

Two advanced ultrasound techniques have emerged as potential tools for non-invasive rejection assessment. Acoustic Radiation Force Impulse (ARFI) elastography quantifies tissue stiffness by measuring shear wave velocity (in m/s): higher velocities indicate stiffer tissue, which may reflect rejection-induced inflammation and fibrosis. Shear wave elastography has been extensively studied in liver transplantation, where increased stiffness correlates with acute and chronic rejection \cite{yoon2013}, and a recent meta-analysis in kidney transplantation found moderate correlations between shear wave velocity and allograft fibrosis \cite{filipov2024}. However, prior to Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025}, no study had systematically evaluated elastography for pancreas graft rejection.

Dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (DCE-US) assesses parenchymal perfusion by tracking the passage of microbubble contrast agent through the graft vasculature. Time-intensity curves are fitted to the enhancement pattern, yielding parameters such as peak enhancement, wash-in area under the curve, and wash-out rate. DCE-US has been applied to kidney transplants, where reduced perfusion correlates with rejection \cite{jin2015, kersting2013}. In the only prior pancreas transplant study, Kersting et al.\ \cite{kersting2013} reported lower perfusion during rejection episodes in 14 transplant recipients (42 total examinations), though the sample was small and the study was single-centre.

Bassaganyas et al.\ \cite{bassaganyas2025} conducted the first prospective study combining both techniques in pancreas transplantation. Using the same patient cohort and imaging protocol as the present thesis, they demonstrated that after the initial 90-day post-operative period, rejection was associated with significantly higher tissue stiffness (ARFI median 1.46 vs 0.97~m/s, $p < 0.001$) and lower graft perfusion (multiple DCE-US parameters reaching $p < 0.05$). The combined score achieved an odds ratio of 23.2 for rejection. These results established that quantitative ultrasound biomarkers can detect rejection non-invasively, motivating the question addressed in this thesis: whether automated texture analysis of the same grayscale images can provide complementary or additional diagnostic information.

\subsection{Radiomics in Medical Imaging}

Radiomics is the high-throughput extraction of quantitative features from medical images, converting imaging data into mineable numeric descriptors of tissue texture, intensity, and spatial heterogeneity \cite{lambin2012}. The fundamental premise is that disease processes alter tissue microstructure in ways that manifest as subtle image texture patterns, even when these changes are imperceptible to visual inspection \cite{gillies2016}. The radiomics workflow typically involves image acquisition, region-of-interest segmentation, image preprocessing, feature extraction, feature selection, and statistical or machine learning analysis.

Standardisation of radiomics feature computation has been addressed by the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI), which defined and standardised 169 radiomics features across multiple feature classes \cite{zwanenburg2020}. The PyRadiomics library implements IBSI-compliant feature extraction and is widely used in the research community \cite{vanGriethuysen2017}. Features are organised into classes capturing different aspects of the image: first-order statistics (intensity histogram properties), gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM, spatial relationships between pixel pairs), gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM, consecutive pixels of the same intensity), gray-level size-zone matrix (GLSZM, connected regions of similar intensity), gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM, pixel neighbourhood dependencies), and neighbouring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM, local intensity differences).

While radiomics has been most extensively validated in oncological applications using CT and MRI, its application to ultrasound imaging presents specific challenges. Ultrasound images are inherently operator-dependent (probe pressure, gain settings, angle of insonation), lack standardised intensity scales (unlike Hounsfield units in CT), and contain speckle noise intrinsic to the coherent imaging process. These factors introduce variability that may obscure disease-related texture patterns: Duron et al.\ \cite{duron2021} showed that preprocessing choices significantly affect feature repeatability in ultrasound radiomics, and Soleymani et al.\ \cite{soleymani2022} found that only 27.6\% of radiomics features were reproducible across different ultrasound scan settings and scanner vendors. Nevertheless, several studies have applied radiomics to ultrasound for liver fibrosis staging, thyroid nodule classification, and breast lesion characterisation \cite{wang2019liver, park2021thyroid, romeo2021breast}, with variable success depending on the degree of acquisition standardisation.

To our knowledge, no prior study has applied radiomics feature extraction to ultrasound images of pancreas transplant grafts. This thesis therefore represents a novel application of an established methodology to an unexplored clinical domain.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Medical Image Classification}

Machine learning classification in medical imaging aims to learn decision boundaries that separate disease states based on extracted features. In the context of radiomics, the task is typically binary classification (e.g., rejection vs.\ no rejection) using a feature matrix where each row is a patient study and each column is a radiomics feature.

Several challenges are characteristic of medical imaging datasets. First, sample sizes are typically small (tens to hundreds of observations) relative to the number of features, creating a high-dimensional setting prone to overfitting \cite{vabalas2019}. Feature selection or dimensionality reduction is therefore essential: common approaches include correlation-based filtering (removing redundant features with high inter-correlation) and univariate selection (retaining features with the strongest individual association with the outcome). Second, class imbalance is frequent, as disease cases are often outnumbered by controls. Techniques such as class-weighted loss functions adjust the model to penalise misclassification of the minority class proportionally. Third, robust evaluation requires careful cross-validation strategies. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) provides a nearly unbiased estimate of generalisation performance but has high variance. Stratified $k$-fold cross-validation balances bias and variance while maintaining class proportions in each fold.

The classifiers most commonly applied to radiomics data include logistic regression (interpretable, linear decision boundary), random forests (ensemble of decision trees, handles non-linear relationships), support vector machines (maximum-margin classifier, effective in high dimensions), and naive Bayes (probabilistic, assumes feature independence) \cite{parmar2015}. Joint optimisation of feature selection and model hyperparameters within a nested cross-validation framework is considered best practice to avoid information leakage between the selection and evaluation steps.

In this thesis, we employ all four classifiers within a joint optimisation pipeline, evaluated by both LOOCV and stratified 10-fold cross-validation on independent data subsets, following current methodological recommendations for small medical imaging datasets.

\section{Objectives}

The main goal of this thesis is to investigate whether radiomics texture features extracted from grayscale ultrasound images can predict pancreas transplant rejection. To this end, the following specific objectives are defined:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Extract radiomics features from pancreas transplant ultrasound images.} Develop a preprocessing pipeline to isolate the pancreas graft region from clinician-annotated ultrasound images, and apply standardised radiomics feature extraction to produce a quantitative description of tissue texture.

\item \textbf{Test whether individual radiomics features discriminate rejection from no rejection.} Conduct statistical hypothesis testing on each extracted feature to determine whether any individual texture or intensity measure differs significantly between the two outcome groups.

\item \textbf{Build machine learning classifiers and evaluate predictive performance.} Train and evaluate multiple classification models using radiomics features as input, assessing whether multivariate combinations of features achieve discriminative performance beyond individual feature analysis.

\item \textbf{Reproduce the statistical analysis of clinical imaging biomarkers.} Apply the same statistical methodology to the clinical imaging features (ARFI elastography and DCE-US perfusion parameters) recorded for the same patient cohort, and compare the results with those previously reported in the literature \cite{bassaganyas2025}.

\item \textbf{Compare automated radiomics with manual clinical biomarkers.} Contrast the discriminative ability of automated texture features with that of manually measured clinical imaging biomarkers, to assess whether radiomics provides additional or complementary diagnostic value.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Structure of the Report}

This report is organised into four chapters. Chapter~1 introduces the clinical problem, reviews related work, and states the objectives. Chapter~2 describes the methods used in this study, including the dataset, the image preprocessing pipeline, radiomics feature extraction, statistical analysis, and the machine learning classification framework. Chapter~3 presents the results of the statistical and machine learning analyses for both radiomics and clinical features. Chapter~4 discusses the findings, addresses limitations of the study, outlines directions for future work, and draws conclusions.
